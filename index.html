About

I live in Los Angeles. I’m a data scientist at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, where I work on NIH-funded studies of the brain, genes and the immune system. I also build AI products. I was the founding engineer of Apateu, a student-housing app with an AI search engine, and I built Moonlight, an iPhone app for parents of children with autism. Now I’m building Stormi, a home robot: it sees with its own cameras, remembers the day, answers when you call its name, and lives in a body I designed and 3D-print. For fun, I’m also training a drone pilot with reinforcement learning.

I grew up in China and studied statistics at Southeast University (东南大学) in Nanjing, then mathematics at Utrecht University and analytics at USC.

MoonlightAI engineer / full-stack software engineer · San Diego · 2026An iPhone app for parents of children with autism. Parents log the day with a photo or a voice note, and the app turns weeks of notes into charts a doctor can read. MoreLess

Parents of a child who can’t always say how they feel keep track of a lot, then get asked to remember weeks of it in a short appointment. Moonlight gives them one place to log the day, and it finds patterns across weeks.

One rule shaped the design: the language model may suggest a pattern, but every number in a report comes from the database, never from the model. The app gives no medical advice. If the model drafts something that sounds like advice, code swaps it for a note to ask a doctor.

I built it end to end, from requirements with the family to version one in production in August 2026, after a four-week build. Since then the family has driven a new round of changes almost every week. SwiftUI, Node, MongoDB, OpenAI and AWS.

Stormi watches, keeps a diary of the day, and answers when you call its name. I build every layer: the vision, the memory, the voice, the iPhone app and the printed body. MoreLess

How it works

SenseTwo cameras, colour and infrared, and four microphones
Notice, on the robotFinds people and pets, reads each body’s pose and follows everyone, in real time
Describe, in the cloudWhen something changes, a few frames go to a vision-language model that writes what happened
Remember, on the robotA diary of the day and a memory of the household, encrypted, fading with age
TalkSay “Stormi” and ask. One agent answers by voice or in the iPhone app

Video stays on the robot · only short moments it chooses go to the cloud

What it watches for

Stormi’s first job is to help families of children with autism see two things: the movements that repeat, and the moments a parent needs to know about. It reads each body as a skeleton and looks for rhythm.

The same diary catches the moments that matter for safety: leaving the room, getting out of bed at night, hitting the head. A parent can ask for them in plain words: “tell me if he leaves the living room.”

An agent that knows the house

Stormi listens only for its name. Then one agent, with the same memory and tools, answers out loud or in the app’s chat: it searches its diary, and looks through its own cameras when it needs to.

Edge and cloud, split on purpose

Small networks on the robot catch every change as it happens and pick the moments worth a look. A pretrained vision-language model in the cloud puts them into words. Without internet, the robot keeps working.

Learns the household, no retraining

Tell Stormi who someone is or correct a line, by tap, chat or voice, and it knows at once. What it learns sits in a memory you can read, fix and delete, not inside a model.

Measured before trusted

Every change gets a pass mark written down before the test, then one score on days it has never seen. I put eight small vision-language models through it; none passed, so the cloud model stays.

An autonomous delivery droneA fun side project · since summer 2026Just for fun: software for a drone that carries a package from A to B around trees, poles and buildings. A pilot trained with reinforcement learning does the flying, and simple safety rules it can’t override have the last word. It flies in simulation; the aircraft itself isn’t built. MoreLess

I wrote a simulator in PyTorch that flies 2,000 virtual drones at once on one GPU, and trained the pilot in it with PPO, a reinforcement-learning method.

The safety rules sit between the pilot and the drone: never fly faster than you can stop, and treat anything the sensors can’t see as blocked. When the pilot found two ways to crash that the rules had missed, I fixed the rules and tested them on 9,840 simulated flights without a crash.

After two hours of training, the pilot reached the goal on 225 of 240 courses it had never seen, with one crash.
