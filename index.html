About

I live in Los Angeles. I’m a data scientist at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, where I work on NIH-funded studies of the brain, genes and the immune system. I also build AI products. I was the founding engineer of Apateu, a student-housing app with an AI search engine, and I built Moonlight, an iPhone app for parents of children with autism. Now I’m building the vision system for a home robot: a small camera that understands what people are doing. For fun, I’m also writing the software for a delivery drone.

I grew up in China and studied statistics at Southeast University (东南大学) in Nanjing, then mathematics at Utrecht University and analytics at USC.

My main project asks whether traffic pollution ages the brain. For it I created MRIreduce, an open-source R package on CRAN that turns brain scans into data a model can use, and then traced which brain regions carry pollution’s effect on memory in about 1,600 older women. I’m first author on the paper, now under revision.

  • Built a deep-learning model that predicts hormone levels from brain scans of about 9,100 teenagers, and shows which brain regions drive each prediction (OHBM 2026 abstract).
  • Built PedsHEAR, a web tool for doctors that estimates hearing-loss risk for children on cisplatin chemotherapy, around my advisor’s model.

MoonlightAI engineer / full-stack software engineer · San Diego · 2026An iPhone app for parents of children with autism. Parents log the day with a photo or a voice note, and the app turns weeks of notes into charts a doctor can read. MoreLess

Parents of a child who can’t always say how they feel keep track of a lot, then get asked to remember weeks of it in a short appointment. Moonlight gives them one place to log the day, and it finds patterns across weeks.

One rule shaped the design: the language model may suggest a pattern, but every number in a report comes from the database, never from the model. The app gives no medical advice. If the model drafts something that sounds like advice, code swaps it for a note to ask a doctor.

I built it end to end, from requirements with the family to version one in production in August 2026, after a four-week build. Since then the family has driven a new round of changes almost every week. SwiftUI, Node, MongoDB, OpenAI and AWS.

Putting AI on hardware

AI that works in the physical world: vision models that run on a small computer with a camera, and a drone pilot trained with reinforcement learning.

A home robot’s eyesAI engineer · since summer 2026A home robot that helps families of children with autism. I’m building its eyes: a small camera computer that understands, second by second, what each person in the room is doing, while the video stays in the home. MoreLess

How it works:

  • Public video first. The model learned everyday actions (sitting, eating, reading, playing) from large public video datasets.
  • People, not just pixels. The camera finds each person, works out their pose and follows them over time. Once a second it sums up each person, and a small transformer reads the last few seconds to decide what they are doing.
  • A big teacher for a small student. A large vision-language model scores clips offline, and my small model learns from it and from human labels. On the benchmark, the student ended up beating its teacher.
  • Built for a tiny computer. It’s designed for a Raspberry Pi 5 with an AI chip. The model I trained needs a few milliseconds of one processor core per person each second.
  • Measured honestly. Each version is scored once, on clips it never trained on, and a model stays only if it beats a simpler one.
An autonomous delivery droneA fun side project · since summer 2026Just for fun: software for a drone that carries a package from A to B around trees, poles and buildings. A pilot trained with reinforcement learning does the flying, and simple safety rules it can’t override have the last word. It flies in simulation; the aircraft itself isn’t built. MoreLess

I wrote a simulator in PyTorch that flies 2,000 virtual drones at once on one GPU, and trained the pilot in it with PPO, a reinforcement-learning method.

The safety rules sit between the pilot and the drone: never fly faster than you can stop, and treat anything the sensors can’t see as blocked. When the pilot found two ways to crash that the rules had missed, I fixed the rules and tested them on 9,840 simulated flights without a crash.

After two hours of training, the pilot reached the goal on 225 of 240 courses it had never seen, with one crash.
